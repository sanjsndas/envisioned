    Design Principles: The Timeless Rules of Good Space

    Great interiors are not about trends. They are about a set of principles that have remained constant for centuries: proportion, light, balance, and material honesty. In this long-form guide, we unpack these ideas with real-world examples.

    Proportion is the relationship between objects and the human body. It’s why a 3-meter ceiling feels grand and a 2.4-meter one feels cozy. Scale, on the other hand, is how those proportions relate to each other. A massive sofa in a small room is a scale problem—not a proportion problem.

    “The room should feel as though it has always been there—even if it was built last year.” — Anonymous architect

    Light is the most underrated material. It shapes how we perceive color, texture, and depth. In northern climates, maximizing daylight is critical; in hotter regions, controlling it is equally important. We’ll look at both.

    Balance doesn’t mean symmetry. It means visual weight. A heavy stone fireplace can be balanced by a large window, a dark wall by a pale floor. These are the subtle moves that make a room feel resolved.

    Material honesty—using materials for what they are—creates authenticity. A concrete wall should look like concrete, not faux stone. Wood should show its grain. This principle is central to modern architecture and enduring interiors.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What is the difference between interior design and interior architecture?
    Interior architecture focuses on the structural and spatial aspects of a building’s interior—walls, lighting, and circulation. Interior design is more about finishes, furniture, and the sensory experience. Both overlap significantly in residential projects.
    How do I choose the right lighting for a small room?
    Use layered lighting: a ceiling fixture for general light, wall sconces or a floor lamp for ambient glow, and a small task light. Avoid a single bright overhead source, which flattens the space.
    Are open floor plans still popular?
    Yes, but with refinements. Many homeowners now prefer “broken plan” layouts—partially separated zones that offer acoustic and visual privacy while maintaining connection.
    What is the most sustainable material for interiors?
    There’s no single answer, but reclaimed wood, bamboo, cork, and lime plaster all have low embodied energy. Locally sourced materials are almost always a better choice than exotic imports.
    How can I make a rental feel like home without major changes?
    Focus on lighting (swap out harsh fixtures with plug-in sconces), removable wallpaper, large rugs, and freestanding furniture. These make a big impact with minimal damage.
    What are the current trends in kitchen design?
    Warm minimalism, handleless cabinets, integrated appliances, and a mix of natural stone and wood. Also, more focus on the kitchen as a social space rather than just a workspace.
